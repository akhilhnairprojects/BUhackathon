08 · Airport upkeep (a case study) and what a layover can be used for¶

Two supporting analyses, both labelled as such in the deck.

Upkeep / renovation. There is no public "condition rating" for airports. FAA Airport Improvement Program grant histories (https://www.faa.gov/airports/aip/grant_histories) record capital investment, but a documented 2025 construction event lets us measure the effect directly: Newark's runway 4L-22R was closed for a $121M rehabilitation from 15 April to 2 June 2025 (Port Authority / FAA; reopened 13 days early), with weeknight closures continuing afterwards. The same spring also saw air-traffic-control staffing problems at Newark, so the effect below is "runway closure plus ATC trouble", not construction alone.

Policy implication: before booking tight connections or time-critical trips through a hub, the travel desk checks the FAA's quarterly airport construction notices (https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/construction) and treats hubs with active runway work like weather-fragile hubs - longer buffer, or route around. This is a watch-list rule, not a ranking of airports by condition: grant histories measure spending, not state of repair.

What can a traveler actually do with a layover?¶

The recommended minimum layover is 90 minutes (notebook 05). Deplaning, walking and boarding take roughly 45-50 of those, leaving ~40 usable minutes - enough for a lounge, a meal or a walk, not a workout. Amenities below were looked up on 6 Nov 2026 from the sources shown; several fitness sources are from 2018 and must be re-checked before they go on a slide.
